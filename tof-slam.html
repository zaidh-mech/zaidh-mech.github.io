Robotics / Final year project

ToF SLAM & Autonomous Mobile Robot

A purpose-built differential-drive robot developed around the mechanical, sensing, electronics and host-software requirements of a low-cost indoor Time-of-Flight SLAM system.

Context
BEng final year project
Period
Dec 2025 - Jul 2026
Role
Individual system design & build
Tools
SolidWorks, KiCad, ESP32, MATLAB, Python

Why I built the robot around the project

I chose not to adapt an existing robot model because the mechanical platform was part of the engineering problem. A generic chassis would have fixed the wheelbase, sensor position and internal volume before the sensing and electronics architecture had been resolved.

Designing the platform from the component level allowed the scanner field of view, drive geometry, battery position, PCB envelope, cable paths and service access to be developed together. It also created a controlled mechanical reference for encoder-based motion and repeatable ToF scanning.

Sensor-first geometry

The front scanner was positioned with an unobstructed view and a defined rotational axis.

Stable drive layout

The wheel, motor and caster positions were set as one differential-drive package.

Integrated electronics

PCB, battery and interfaces were packaged into the chassis instead of added after it.

Build access

Layered plates and a separate shell keep internal parts reachable during assembly and iteration.

Design criteria

A compact platform that could be built, tested and changed.

The chassis was developed against practical system requirements rather than appearance alone.

01

Compactness

02

Maintainability

Assembly integration

Mechanical and electrical packaging were resolved together.

The open assembly was used to check the real occupied volume, mounting relationships and access before the housing was fitted.

Mechanical decisions

The battery was positioned centrally over the drive axis to support traction and reduce the chance of wheel slip affecting odometry. Electronics and sensing hardware were placed on the upper deck, keeping the drive package below and simplifying access during development.

The VL53L8CX has a limited field of view, so it was mounted on a 28BYJ-48 stepper-driven scanner. The stepper provided repeatable angular increments, while a homing sensor established a consistent zero reference after startup.

Software integration

One workflow from operator command to mapped environment.

The robot was developed as a distributed system. The ESP32 handles time-critical hardware tasks, a Python GUI provides the operator interface, and MATLAB converts timestamped motion and range data into the live SLAM map.

How the software fits the robot

The software was separated by responsibility so that motor safety and sensor timing remain on the robot while heavier mapping and simulation tasks run on the computer.

The ESP32 reads wheel encoders, the MPU6050 and the scanning VL53L8CX, then forms timestamped odometry and range packets. The Python interface selects either the physical robot or the MATLAB simulation, sends motion and scan commands, and exposes encoder, IMU and ToF diagnostics during development.

For physical operation, MATLAB receives the robot telemetry, reconstructs motion from cumulative encoder ticks and IMU yaw, aligns each ToF measurement with its acquisition pose, and updates the occupancy map and SLAM estimate. The same control vocabulary is accepted by the MATLAB software-in-the-loop simulator, allowing the command and mapping workflow to be checked before operating the hardware.
